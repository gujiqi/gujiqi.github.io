Research

I study how environmental filtering, functional traits, dispersal and evolutionary history shape biodiversity across space and time. My work combines field ecology, global databases and quantitative models, from bryophyte community assembly to historical biogeography and multi-taxon community dynamics.

Trait-based biodiversity and community assembly

Functional traits connect biodiversity patterns to the ecological strategies that may produce them. I am developing WorldBryDat, a global bryophyte trait resource covering 6,123 species and approximately 264 trait fields: 195 morphological, 12 reproductive, 49 life-history and 8 habitat-preference traits [31]. The database is intended to make these often scattered observations easier to compare across taxa, environments and lineages, and to support analyses that link species traits with community composition and environmental gradients.

Tibetan bryophytes: field ecology and community assembly

My current community-assembly work on the Tibetan Plateau brings together 551 field plots, records for 1,008 species, 264 traits and phylogenetic information in a hierarchical modelling framework [28]. The sampling spans six geographic regions shown on the map. By combining field observations with trait, evolutionary and environmental data, the study asks how environmental filtering, trait differentiation and evolutionary conservatism contribute to bryophyte community composition across this varied region.

This larger synthesis builds on earlier field studies at different scales. A survey of 184 plots and 324 species compared forest, thicket and herbaceous communities: richness was highest in forests, while the balance between deterministic and stochastic assembly processes shifted among vegetation types; soil pH, climate and soil conditions were important correlates [19]. Across 26 sites and elevations of 3,642–5,528 m, measurements of 23 traits in the xerophytic moss Indusiella thianschanica showed substantial within-species variation, with temperature, elevation and potential evapotranspiration among the main environmental correlates [16].

A regional habitat model for Polytrichaceae used field records and optimized MaxEnt models to identify potential habitat, highlighting temperature, elevation, precipitation and vegetation cover, with the most suitable areas concentrated in southeastern Tibet [15]. In alpine forests, surveys of 72 trees and 1,152 quadrats showed that host-tree identity and elevation help explain epiphytic bryophyte diversity [10]. My field programme also includes liverwort communities along Sygera Mountain’s elevational gradient, where richness showed two peaks and community composition varied with elevation and climatic conditions [13]. Work on the endemic moss Encalypta buxbaumioidea uses species-distribution modelling to examine its potential range and conservation exposure under future climate scenarios [12].

Together, these studies move from local substrate and host-tree relationships to regional distribution and community assembly, while keeping the small spatial scale at which bryophytes experience their environment in view. Taxonomic work has documented a newly recorded bryophyte family in Tibet [11], two newly recorded hornwort species [23], and a molecularly supported mainland-China record and biogeographic analysis of Pogonatum tahitense [14]. I have also contributed to studies of moss community structure in Qinhuangdao and desert-plant diversity and phylogenetic structure in Xinjiang [18, 21].

Eco-evolutionary modelling and historical biogeography

Present-day ecological models are usually fitted to contemporary communities, even though the environments, geographic connections and evolutionary lineages that shape those communities have changed over geological time. I am developing HmscEE to extend joint species distribution modelling into this deep-time setting, with a framework designed to represent biodiversity across a changing Earth over the past 540 million years [30].

The framework brings together community observations, functional traits and phylogenies with fossil evidence, palaeoenvironmental reconstructions, changing land and sea geography, and dispersal opportunities. Its process structure is intended to connect environmental and biotic filtering with dispersal, evolution, speciation and extinction, while representing the habitat and movement constraints created by past geography. The outputs include spatial summaries of lineage richness and diversity alongside uncertainty and diagnostics that help distinguish supported predictions from extrapolation.

The bryophyte case study is being assembled from approximately 4.4 million occurrence records and spans roughly 515 million years of the group’s history. It provides a test bed for asking how changing climate, available habitat, dispersal routes and lineage history can be considered together. HmscEE remains a framework and manuscript in development; the work is focused on establishing the modelling approach and its limits rather than presenting a completed reconstruction as settled evidence. My related methods work includes a review and practical tutorial on joint species distribution models, with particular attention to the HMSC framework, its integration of environment, traits, phylogeny and spatial structure, and the limits of interpreting residual species associations [24]. I am also contributing to JSDM Studio, software intended to make joint species-distribution analyses more accessible [29]. A separate co-authored article examines how large language models could support biodiversity research, from literature review and hypothesis development to study design, data analysis and scientific writing, while discussing the need for expert oversight, accuracy checks and responsible use [17].

Global biodiversity dynamics and species distribution modelling

LIFEPLAN offers a repeated, standardized view of biodiversity across globally distributed sites and multiple taxonomic groups. In an ongoing analysis, I am examining whether urbanization changes the pathway of community change, as well as turnover, recurrence and temporal stability. The data represented in this project include 3,426 bird species and 462,956 molecular operational taxonomic units across arthropods and fungi: 323,338 arthropod, 72,562 soil-fungal and 67,056 airborne-fungal OTUs [27]. Because these groups are sampled with different methods and at different numbers of sites, the analysis treats each stream in its sampling context rather than assuming identical coverage. A second strand examines how biodiversity predictions depend on the distribution data and validation choices used to build species distribution models. For 233 marine fish species, a comparison of expert range maps with opportunistic occurrence records found that the two inputs can produce different predictions; occurrence-based models transferred better to regions without calibration records in that study [22]. A related study evaluates how to integrate expert maps and occurrence records in marine-fish range estimates [26]. For tropical reef fishes, a multi-species assessment of 1,941 taxa found that predictive performance was associated with geographic and environmental characteristics of species and with similarity between training and evaluation environments [25]. Work on 60 goatfish species further showed that random and spatial cross-validation often selected different model settings and yielded different distribution projections [20]. Together, these contributions focus on a practical issue in macroecology and conservation: a distribution map is only as reliable as the data and validation design behind it.

Earlier empirical and applied ecology

My earlier work connects ecological field questions with environmental assessment and quantitative tools. In bryophyte ecology, early conference work explored neural-network methods for community research [6], followed by studies of epiphytic bryophyte distributions and host-tree patterns on Sejila Mountain [7, 9]. In soil science, I examined how freeze–thaw cycling relates to available nitrogen in Mollisol profiles [5].

I also contributed to applied water-environment research, including water-quality assessment in the Songhua River basin, ecological-risk monitoring for the Nenjiang River basin, and the use of artificial-intelligence and augmented-reality approaches in environmental monitoring and assessment [1–4, 8]. These projects provided experience with field observations, environmental data and applied modelling. They sit alongside my current ecological work as part of a broader interest in building quantitative evidence that can help describe environmental patterns and inform biodiversity assessment.
