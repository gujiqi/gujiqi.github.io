Research

Bryophyte traits and community assembly

A central part of my research is building data and field evidence to understand how bryophyte traits relate to the environments species occupy and the communities they form. I am developing WorldBryDat, a global database covering 6,123 species and approximately 264 trait fields: 195 morphological, 12 reproductive, 49 life-history and 8 habitat-preference traits [31]. By bringing scattered species-level information into a consistent resource, WorldBryDat is designed to support comparisons of ecological strategies across lineages and environments, and to make trait data more usable in community-level analyses.

My current Tibetan Plateau synthesis brings together 551 plots, 1,008 species, 264 traits, phylogenetic information and environmental measurements [28]. It asks how environmental filtering, trait differentiation and evolutionary conservatism jointly shape bryophyte community composition across six geographic regions. This combination of plot-level sampling and species-level data lets the study examine assembly processes across a broad environmental gradient, while distinguishing environmental responses from patterns associated with shared evolutionary history. The manuscript is under review.

This synthesis builds on my first-author study of 184 plots and 324 species across forest, thicket and herbaceous vegetation in Tibet [19]. The study compared richness and community composition among vegetation types and used complementary analyses to assess environmental and stochastic contributions to assembly. Forests supported the highest richness, while the relative importance of stochastic processes increased from forest to thicket and herbaceous communities; soil conditions, including pH, and climate helped explain the observed differences. In a second first-author field study, I examined epiphytic bryophytes on 72 trees using 1,152 quadrats along Sygera Mountain [10]. Richness followed a non-linear elevational pattern, peaking around 3,500 m; it was greater on the eastern than western slope and differed among host trees, with Quercus semecarpifolia supporting the greatest diversity. Together, these studies show why bryophyte diversity needs to be examined at both community and substrate scales: regional climate and elevation set broad conditions, while host identity and tree properties shape local habitat.

Models for community ecology and biodiversity

My first-author methodological work addresses how ecologists can make community-level inference from data on many species at once. In a 2026 review and practical tutorial, I explain the statistical foundations of joint species distribution models, with a focus on the Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities (HMSC) framework [24]. The paper walks through the full workflow, from organizing community, environmental, trait and phylogenetic data to model specification, Bayesian estimation, evaluation, interpretation and prediction. A reproducible bryophyte example shows how these steps can be applied in practice. The review also emphasizes an important interpretive limit: residual species associations can reflect unmeasured environmental factors or other shared structure, so they should not be treated as direct proof of species interactions.

I am also contributing to JSDM Studio, software intended to help researchers build and explore joint species distribution models [29]. In a separate first-author perspective, I examined potential uses of large language models across biodiversity research, including literature synthesis, hypothesis development, study design, data analysis and scientific writing [17]. That paper considers both opportunities and constraints, particularly the need for domain expertise, verification of generated information and careful attention to research quality and ethics. These projects share a practical aim: making quantitative methods and emerging tools useful to ecologists while keeping their assumptions and limitations visible.

Historical biogeography across deep time

Ecological communities are shaped by a changing Earth: climates shift, habitats appear and disappear, continents move, and lineages diversify or go extinct. I am developing HmscEE, a spatially explicit framework that extends joint species distribution modelling to investigate biodiversity over geological time, with a modelling scope of approximately 540 million years [30]. It is designed to bring together species occurrences, functional traits and phylogenies with fossil evidence, palaeoenvironmental reconstructions, changing continental geography and dispersal opportunities.

The framework links these inputs to ecological and evolutionary processes, including environmental and biotic filtering, dispersal, evolution, speciation and extinction. It also derives spatial layers for habitat availability, movement resistance and plate-carrying transformations, then summarizes outputs such as lineage richness, diversity, uncertainty and areas where predictions extend beyond the evidence. The purpose is to make changing geography and habitat explicit when interpreting long-term biodiversity patterns, rather than treating present-day spatial relationships as fixed through time.

The bryophyte application is being assembled from approximately 4.4 million occurrence records and covers roughly 515 million years of bryophyte history. This case study is intended to test how far occurrence, trait, phylogenetic, fossil and palaeoenvironmental evidence can be integrated in one analytical framework. HmscEE and its manuscript are in development, so I present it as an ongoing methodological programme rather than a completed historical reconstruction.

Global biodiversity change and distribution models

In my ongoing first-author LIFEPLAN study, I am testing whether urbanization changes the pathway of community change, rather than only its rate [27]. The project draws on repeated observations across four data streams: 3,426 bird species and 462,956 molecular operational taxonomic units (OTUs), including 323,338 arthropod, 72,562 soil-fungal and 67,056 airborne-fungal OTUs. I examine community turnover, recurrence and temporal stability while accounting for differences in sampling methods and site coverage among these groups. The analysis is in progress; the central contribution will be to assess how urbanization relates to the form and repeatability of biodiversity change across taxa.

My collaborative species-distribution work examines how data source, model evaluation and geographic transfer affect range estimates. Studies of marine fishes compare expert range maps with occurrence records and assess how those inputs can be integrated [22, 26]. Further analyses evaluate predictive performance across tropical reef-fish species and compare random with spatial cross-validation, showing why validation design matters when models are used to predict into new places [20, 25].

Field collaborations and earlier applied ecology

Collaborative bryophyte studies extend this work to trait variation, habitat suitability and regional community patterns. These include environmental correlates of within-species trait variation in the xerophytic moss Indusiella thianschanica [16], distribution and habitat preferences of Polytrichaceae in Tibet [15], and liverwort diversity along Sygera Mountain [13]. Other collaborations address climate exposure of an endemic moss [12], bryophyte records and taxonomy in Tibet and China [11, 14, 23], and community or phylogenetic patterns in mosses and desert plants [18, 21]. These contributions broaden the taxonomic and geographic scope of my research while complementing the first-author field studies described above.

My first-author soil study examined how freeze–thaw cycling affects available nitrogen through the plough-layer profiles of Mollisol [5]. Earlier work also introduced me to quantitative ecology through a conference study of neural-network methods for bryophyte research [6], alongside field investigations of epiphytic bryophytes on Sejila Mountain [7, 9]. I have also written sole-author chapters on artificial intelligence and augmented reality in environmental assessment and water-environment applications [4, 8], and contributed to applied studies of river water quality, ecological-risk monitoring and environmental information systems [1–3].
